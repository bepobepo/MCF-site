Twenty Million Trees

Moshe Raphaely · Printed pages 70, 72, 74

Dorian placed the call on speaker before wiping the sweat off his face. A stifled breeze was coming from Clissold Park, a parched, dry hum.

‘Is it hot in London too?’ his father asked. He began reciting the forecast, confident he alone has access to it.

The streets were hollowed by the heat and his father’s crisp voice sounded at once booming and intimate, as if they were the only people left on the planet. ‘Thirty-four degrees in London tomorrow, thirty-six on Sunday!’

They often discussed the weather, at length, and selected oddity news – a woman from Milton Keynes who woke up one day speaking in a perfect Welsh accent, or a video of migrating swifts forming a dotted whale in the sky. It was their weekly call; Dorian had few habits and even the most tedious were cherished with zeal.

His father was particularly excited this week: scientists had discovered that the root system of the old weald forest was still alive, or that it could be revived if conditions were right. The edges of the woods ran parallel to his childhood home and his dad had told him many times how the last oaks were cut down 600 years ago.

Dorian used to walk all the way to the ocean, amazed that the land could forget so completely. Blackberry bushes lining roads that smelled like farms, but not a single tree in all of the phantom forest.

His father had emailed the scientists, pledging his support as a local. He was uncommonly emotional that day, undoubtedly because of the heat. ‘Your mum would have loved to hear this.’ he paused to clear his voice. ‘It had never before occurred to me I might see it blossom again.’

It was then that Dorian saw the notification on his phone, about a party in a disused factory in Hackney Wick.

He said goodbye to his father and stared at the message. He had seen the invite earlier and was unmoved, but now it seemed vital to go to a party, any party.

He took a shower and got dressed. The nature of his powers being as it was, Dorian rarely hesitated when making a decision and never regretted it. It was an instinct that grew out of resignation, not spite, from knowing that the world could change anytime he closed his eyes.

In truth, they weren't his powers as much as he was theirs. No audience could witness them, and Dorian couldn’t control them. Yes, he had to close his eyes — and for a period of his childhood he tried to keep them open for as long as he was awake — but he never knew when the world would rearrange itself.

A stone where an oak tree stood, a girl's name, the colour of her eyes. Most changes were minute but they were all complete as the flood, and Dorian lonelier than Noah. The only one still in possession of both the extinct and the new.

He moved to London after graduation and got a job at the London archives where he worked hard but never stayed longer than necessary, and mostly passed on his colleagues’ invitations for drinks. Pale and wiry with marbled-yellow eyes — attractive, if not handsome — Dorian assumed the looks his powers commanded.

Because he knew relationships were mortal, he took on the world with a mix of desperation and indifference:

he could spend seventy straight hours with strangers, taking any drug or drink they offered and listening to their deepest secrets, then withdraw to his flat, never having asked for their names; he bought the best food, the nicest gifts, but cared very little for money, and his own flat was sparsely decorated; of sleep, the longest of his departures from the world, he was chary.

In his dreams he visited cities speckled with beautiful spell-singing women. Sometimes they were naked and he would kiss them, but most times they would wash over him incuriously. When he would wake up he would wonder if any of these women existed, in any world, or whether he had just created them. He never saw the same woman twice.

◆◆◆

He walked down the River Lea towpath and then turned right into Hackney Wick. He recognised the streets from the WW2 bombsite maps at the archives, hand-coloured: Wyke Road was yellow (blast damage, minor in nature), the result of a bomb on nearby purple Monier Road (damaged beyond repair). He had never seen this version of the streets, and their concreteness disoriented him. He stopped for a pint and watched the light drain. In a mural across the street, a young woman was dreamily resting her head on the broad shoulders of a masked superhero, thinking: ‘From the moment we met, I knew the reasons why I’d leave you.’

It was closer to midnight when he finally rang the bell. A bearded man opened the door and immediately hugged him, inhaling and exhaling with great intention. A girl was looking at them, smiling, and Dorian thought she was laughing at the scene, perhaps because she knew the bearded man. But her smile was patient and her large eyes fixed on Dorian readily.

She was tall and lithe, like an athlete or a circus performer. Dorian smiled back, their shared gaze bobbing up and down to the rhythm of the bold man’s profound breaths.

‘You're Dorian, right?' His lips parted slightly, his eyes narrowed.

'Don't worry, we haven't had sex.' She took a deep breath, but didn't say anything for a few seconds, staring at him instead. 'But I remember you. You have that kind of face, like you've been on the news. Have you been on the news?'

'No,' Dorian said.

'I'm Rosa.' She hugged him, both her hands firmly against his back. 'We did this part once already.'

Then he remembered. He had met her and her partner, Slavko, at a rave in the marshes. Dorian spoke mostly with him. Rosa was dancing, her sequin jumpsuit reflecting reds and yellows from LED strips hanging on trees.

She looked very happy, her moon eyes shut, and Dorian uncoiled when she came over to say hello. After they had hugged, he thought she was high on MD.

‘Is Slavi here too?’ Dorian asked just as the music muted momentarily.

She waved her finger at him, but her smile grew. ’So Slavi you remember?’

They broke up a few months ago, Rosa told him, but they were still in touch. ‘He almost came to the party. We would have ended up having sex. It probably would’ve been weird.’ More people entered, forcing them closer together and deeper into the room.

‘Do you know those situations? When you are with someone but you’re also with whatever memory you have of them? A fantasy, I guess.’

Fantasy was a word Dorian thought about often; it was welcome company.

‘Next you’ll say all love is fantasy,’ he said.

‘I won’t,’ she said, and outstared him to a smile.

She worked with people with addictions, responsible for three men and two women, whom she met daily. She liked listening to people, and that was most of the work anyway. Listening, making sure they took their medications, that they kept their appointments. ‘It’s very small work.’

‘Hardly! You’re practically their mother.’

‘It’s a job.’ She shrugged and Dorian was quick to smile and brush her shoulders with a theatrical double stroke. Rosa laughed and shook her head. ’Blah, blah, blah.’ He could smell orange on her breath, the warmth of it hovering between them.

They kissed in the middle of the dancefloor, but not a single person touched them. If this were all there was to it, Dorian thought, he would be happy. To be held by Rosa and remember all the lovers he once held. He felt the room’s gaze, admiring two lovers kissing to an insubordinate rhythm. Two lovers in a room full of dancers, a perilous feeling similar to destiny.

When he opened his eyes, reluctantly, nothing had changed. When he was younger Dorian used to celebrate such moments, which he took as augury, and even though experience had taught him otherwise, he was celebrating now nonetheless. He couldn't deny that love, or even the memory of love, was an overwhelming grace.

◆◆◆

They stepped out for a smoke, settling themselves on a memorial bench whose dedication was illegible. Dorian pulled on his cigarette hesitantly, barely keeping it lit.

Rosa was telling him about her childhood in Nantlle, population 200, at the foot of Snowdonia. They had moved there from Liverpool when she was seven, because her parents craved rural living. ‘They love it there.

The rain, the moss, the slates. A deafening silence vibe.’ She was talking with great ease, almost singing, a liquid fluency which was a tonic for Dorian

‘And you didn’t like that?’

‘You notice everything in a place like that. Light, sound. Even people’s emotions.’ She paused. ‘Maybe not everyone, but I did. It was a bit much.’

‘Must be lonely,’ Dorian said, ‘being the only witness.’

She finished her cigarette, and Dorian held his out as an offer. They pulled on it in turns, passing it between their lips until the last of its ambers dulled. From the slow-running river came a warm rust-scented mist, and they both were careful not to speak. He suddenly thought of his father planting new trees in the ancient ground of the Weald forest, one tiny acorn at a time; such an unlikely hope. But he didn't say anything about that. It was an evening made for smoking quietly with lovers, warm and still, so fragile he could barely breathe.

From the bench, they walked towards the marshes. A rising nimbus moon coloured the streets in greys and blues.

‘When we were kids,’ she said, ‘before going to bed, we would go to the woods in this light. Me and my sister and my mum.’

‘Like a fairy tale.’

‘We had head torches but when we were coming back, my mum would close them. She’d get us to find our way back in the dark.’

‘Weren’t you afraid?’

‘At the beginning, yes. Very scared. But my mother was there saying no, listen. Just listen.’

A fox screamed, but his cry was silenced by cars over the Eastway. Ahead, behind the treeline, a gossamer blanket of fog covered the field, witches tucking in its folds.

‘Then we got more comfortable. We accepted it – the wind, the animal sounds. We were just like the other creatures living there.’

They walked onto a glistening gravel path, the ground gurgling beneath them, Dorian and Rosa, as well as young Rosa, her sister and mum. In such a large group, the silence hung heavy. ‘Is that why you’re so happy?’

Dorian finally asked.

Rosa placed her hands over his eyes. ‘Close them,’ she whispered. This wasn't how he wanted it to end, but he nodded. He began cataloguing the world before it ceases: the distant cars, the crows, the leaves clapping like falling rain.

Rosa took her hands off his face, but he could still smell her, the orange on her breath. He briefly imagined he was surrounded by swirling dancers again. Then she took his hand and they started walking. He kept recording:

a bright passing light; the rushing river; a flickering moth; the flowers of linden trees, like ripe summer fruit.

Sometimes all that changed was a memory, but memories bled into sensations, changing them for worse, or better. An odd terror was spreading over him. Terror like a pleasure, bottomless and cool as a basalt spring.

He noticed her steps were slower now and uncertain.

‘Are your eyes closed too?’

‘Yes.’ Rosa mumbled. She tightened her hand over his and he could hear her smile. ‘We are falling off the face of the earth.’

Rosa’s hair brushing on his shoulder, her body, the thumping of her heart. A wind in the trees and then on their faces, unexpectedly warm, smelling of farms and blackberry bushes. They both stopped. The silence was so wide that Dorian imagined he had died.

Behind his closed eyes, the rising sun was splashing purple streaks across the sky. He moved slowly to search for her lips in the darkness, but he never paused, confident that the dead cannot desire like him.

Twenty million trees were climbing from the ground, like worshippers begging the heavens, desperate for grace. In the cool morning air he could feel it already, from London to the sea, twenty million trees.
